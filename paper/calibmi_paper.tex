\begin{abstract}
We audit LaBraM and CBraMod on motor imagery under a validation-locked protocol in which preprocessing, architecture, optimization, freeze depth, checkpoint, temperature, and method selection are determined using training-session data only. In four-class BCI Competition IV-2a, every supervised comparator evaluated here outperforms every foundation-model configuration, including validation-selected fine-tuning. However, could this gap be confounded by the differing input pipelines the two model types require? Retraining 3 supervised architectures on the same broadband arrays consumed by the foundation models shows that the effect of switching to broadband input varies in sign across architectures (narrowband minus broadband accuracy of \textminus0.078 for ATCNet, but 0.088 for EEG Conformer). None of the 3 terms is significant after correcting for multiple comparisons (nine subjects), and a direct subject-paired contrast of these architecture-specific terms only shows a difference between ATCNet and the other 2 comparators. This comparison is based on only one split/seed, so the differing signs are merely exploratory, but they suggest that evaluating a single comparator may not be sufficient to separate pretraining and supervised training contributions to the performance in an architecture-invariant manner. Finally, we do not observe the same 4-class deficit when training on a 2-class motor imagery dataset (BNCI2014-004), and we use temperature scaling fitted on a validation set to bring the foundation model's calibration error down to the range of supervised models despite its lower accuracy for the 4-class task.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Electroencephalography (EEG) is a non-invasive method of measuring neural activity. It has been utilized for brain--computer interfaces (BCIs), which translate neural signals into actionable commands for assistive devices (e.g., prosthetics, functional electrical stimulation (FES) therapy) \cite{lotte2018review}. A common BCI task is predicting \emph{motor imagery}, where imagined movements produce distinct neural patterns (e.g., ``right-hand raise'' vs.\ ``left-footed kick'') \cite{pfurtscheller1999erd,lotte2018review}. However, decoding neural recordings remains difficult due to variability across participants, settings, and time, and collecting EEG data is costly \cite{lotte2018review,brunner2024bcifes}.
These constraints make generalizable neural representations imperative, and they have been the focus of recent EEG foundation models, which are pretrained on large collections of neural recordings. Such models (e.g., LaBraM \cite{jiang2024labram}, CBraMod \cite{wang2025cbramod}) learn representations that reduce dependence on task-specific labeled data, and demonstrate transfer across participants and tasks. However, these models have shown inconsistent benefits over standard supervised decoders in recent benchmarks \cite{kontras2026neuroatlas,zare2026negativecontrol}. As foundation models are poised to become more prevalent in neural decoding, their improvements over supervised decoders must be reliable enough to justify their inherent complexity and higher cost of training.
Evaluating these improvements is also difficult, as different model types (foundation vs.\ supervised) require different input pipelines (e.g., filtering, sampling rate, scaling), so an observed performance gap may reflect pipeline differences rather than differences in the learned representations \cite{kontras2026neuroatlas}. Furthermore, the impact of a preprocessing pipeline may differ by the supervised model chosen for comparison, so a single baseline may not be sufficient to establish the value of pretraining.
Here, we perform a validation-locked audit of LaBraM and CBraMod on motor imagery classification. (1) Using only data from the training session to select methods and models, we compare the encoders of these foundation models to three architectures trained in a supervised fashion, as well as architecture-matched controls with random initialization. (2) We retrain the supervised architectures on the same broadband inputs used by the foundation models to determine whether the matched-input accuracy difference is consistent across comparators. (3) We evaluate whether the accuracy deficit is task-dependent, and how improved probability calibration relates to classification accuracy.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{EEG foundation models.}
LaBraM \cite{jiang2024labram} and CBraMod \cite{wang2025cbramod} are transformer encoders pretrained on large collections of unlabeled EEG. Recent benchmarks include AdaBrain-Bench \cite{wu2025adabrainbench}, EEG-FM-Bench \cite{xiong2025eegfmbench}, Brain4FMs \cite{shen2026brain4fms}, NeuralBench \cite{banville2026neuralbench}, NeuroAtlas \cite{kontras2026neuroatlas}, OmniEEG-Bench \cite{lu2026omnieegbench}, and Zare's negative-control analysis \cite{zare2026negativecontrol}. Their conclusions are not uniform. In particular, recent audits already use matched random-weight encoders \cite{kontras2026neuroatlas,banville2026neuralbench,zare2026negativecontrol}; we adopt that control rather than claim it as new.

\textbf{Input pipelines as a confound.}
NeuroAtlas notes that EEG-specific preprocessing may affect reported results \cite{kontras2026neuroatlas}. We measure the corresponding input-matching difference by retraining each supervised comparator on the broadband arrays supplied to the foundation models. Because the pathways differ in band, sampling rate, filter design, notching, scaling, and validation-selected retuning, we call the quantity the \emph{matched-input term}, not an isolated preprocessing effect.

\textbf{Supervised motor-imagery decoders.}
Classical common spatial pattern (CSP) and filter-bank CSP remain established references \cite{ramoser2000csp,ang2008fbcsp}, while EEGNet \cite{lawhern2018eegnet} is a widely used compact neural baseline. Our evaluated comparator set is ShallowConvNet \cite{schirrmeister2017deep}, ATCNet \cite{altaheri2023atcnet}, and EEG Conformer \cite{song2023conformer}.


\section{Validation-Locked Protocol}

\subsection{Selection and Enforcement}

To eliminate data leakage in the selection of hyperparameters and preprocessing, a strict separation of training and held-out sessions was maintained. All tunable choices were determined using only training session data, including preprocessing hyperparameters, standardization statistics, model architecture, probe regularization, learning rate, fine-tuning freeze depth, stopping epoch, temperature, and downstream task thresholds. Each row of results used for reporting is annotated with whether labels from the held-out session were used to select it. This is written by the runner, and checked in the code that assembles the tables: if absent or true, results for that artifact are refused.

\subsection{Splits, Inputs, and Provenance}

For BCI IV 2a, all 9 subjects were used. After rejecting artifacts (epochs exceeding 100\,$\mu$V peak-to-peak, narrowband supervised pathway only), subject 9 had 221, 56, and 264 trials in the fit, selection, and held-out sets, respectively; the other subjects had 230, 58, and 288 trials. The broadband pathway, including both foundation models, applied no amplitude rejection, so subject 9's matched-input terms compare slightly different trial sets. Excluding subject 9 leaves all three signs unchanged. The fit and selection splits were a stratified 80/20\% split of the training session, (seed 42). For BNCI2014004, sessions with fold \texttt{train} were the training session (3) and sessions with fold \texttt{test} were the held-out session (2).

Epochs were 0--4.5 seconds after the cue. For supervised models, EEG was infinite impulse response (IIR) filtered 8--30\,Hz at 250\,Hz and standardized by channel with statistics from the fit split. For foundation models, EEG was zero-phase finite impulse response (FIR) filtered 0.1--75\,Hz at 250\,Hz with a 50\,Hz notch, resampled to 200\,Hz, and cropped from the cue to the nearest whole second, yielding 4 seconds (800 samples) of EEG. Voltages were converted to microvolts and divided by 100 at the encoder boundary, consistent with the reference implementations. For supervised models trained using the foundation model preprocessing, the input was the same array, standardized by channel with statistics from the fit split. Frozen probes were an L2-penalized logistic regression with $C$ selected from [0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10], fit on the features of the encoder. For CBraMod, mean pooling across channels and patches or flattening was selected on the selection split. For LaBraM, the \texttt{fc\_norm} layer, mean-pooled, was used. Fine-tuning trained the encoder together with its linear classification head. Foundation models use learned electrode representations, so require a canonical montage. The montage was reconstructed and checked for all files. All runs were annotated with the device, software versions, and whether they were run deterministically. Results from different devices are not pooled. Frozen probe calculations were run deterministically on CPU. Supervised comparators and fine-tuning were run on Apple's Metal Performance Shaders (MPS) device, which is not bitwise deterministic. In Section~\ref{sec:robust}, a rerun of the important comparison on a deterministic CPU run is provided.

\section{Experimental Design}

\subsection{Models and Adaptation}

We evaluate LaBraM-base (5.8M parameters) \cite{jiang2024labram} and CBraMod (5.0M) \cite{wang2025cbramod}. LaBraM is ported from the reference release; CBraMod uses the community Braindecode implementation, reducing dependence on a local reimplementation. Two additional implementations were attempted but are treated as scope rather than negative evidence: Braindecode LaBraM rejects our input length because of its temporal-embedding configuration, and EEGPT's public interface did not expose the interpolation target needed for our input format.

Supervised comparator families are ShallowConvNet \cite{schirrmeister2017deep}, ATCNet \cite{altaheri2023atcnet}, and EEG Conformer \cite{song2023conformer}, each tuned on the selection split. For the foundation models we evaluate frozen probing and full/partial fine-tuning. Fine-tuning selects learning rate, freeze depth, warm-up behavior, and stopping epoch on validation data, with layer-wise decay 0.9, weight decay 0.05, label smoothing 0.1, batch size 16, and a 60-epoch maximum. Validation favors ten frozen LaBraM blocks at $10^{-4}$ but full CBraMod unfreezing at $5\times10^{-4}$, illustrating that ``foundation-model fine-tuning'' is not one transferable recipe.

\subsection{Controls}

\textbf{Architecture-matched random initialization.}
We train the same encoder architecture from random weights. This allows us to decouple two reasons for poor performance: (i) the encoder weights do not contain useful information for the downstream task, (ii) the encoder architecture cannot be easily trained with a few hundred trials. To ensure that we correctly load the checkpoints, we verify that 178 out of 179 tensors differ from a random initialization.

\textbf{Matched input.} We retrain ShallowConvNet, ATCNet, and EEG Conformer on the same (broadband) array used for the foundation models. For each arm, we select the stopping epoch using the Brier score on the selection split. We train all models for 200 epochs with a batch size of 32. For ShallowConvNet, we additionally select the temporal kernel size and the number of filters on the selection split, per subject, out of four settings (kernel sizes 16, 32, 51 with 40 filters, as well as the narrowband default of 64 with 16 filters). For ATCNet and the EEG Conformer, we use the same architecture as described in their respective papers and select only the learning rate from $\{10^{-2}, 10^{-3}, 3\times10^{-4}\}$. All hyperparameters are selected independently for each pathway. Note that the matched-input term therefore also includes retuning the architectures, and should not be interpreted as solely attributable to individual filters or preprocessing operations.

\subsection{Datasets and Statistics}

The primary dataset used is BCI Competition IV dataset 2a (IV-2a) from~\cite{brunner2008graz,tangermann2012competition}. It has nine subjects with two recording sessions each and four motor imagery classes. There are 22 EEG channels. As a second data setting, we evaluate on dataset BNCI2014-004 from~\cite{leeb2007bnci004}, which contains nine subjects, two classes, and three electrodes (C3, Cz, C4). While there also is dataset BNCI2014-001, it is reconstructed from the same recordings as BCI IV-2a and hence does not provide an independent replication. The comparison between the two datasets is not a controlled ablation of the number of classes, as the dataset and number of channels also change.

The primary metric used for evaluating the quality of the probabilities is the Brier score. In addition, we report accuracy, the expected calibration error (ECE), and the negative log-likelihood (NLL)~\cite{brier1950verification,gneiting2007proper}. For the ECE, we use the top label ECE with 15 equal-mass bins. We compute the metrics per subject on the held-out session and report the mean, along with 95\% confidence intervals estimated using percentile bootstraps with 10{,}000 bootstrap resamples over the nine participants. For temperature scaling~\cite{guo2017calibration}, we fit the temperature on the validation data. We cap it at 20.0, and report if the cap was saturated for a subject, indicating that the raw logits were extremely overconfident. For comparisons between two methods, we conduct a two-sided, subject-paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test~\cite{wilcoxon1945individual}. We correct for multiple hypothesis testing using the Benjamini--Hochberg procedure~\cite{benjamini1995fdr} within each family of tests where we report a primary value. For the $n = 9$ participants in our experiments, the lowest achievable two-sided exact p-value for the Wilcoxon signed-rank test is $2^{1-n} = 0.0039$. We distinguish between not finding a difference between two methods, and finding evidence that they are equal.

\subsection{Fine-Tuning Stability}

The initial reference recipe hyperparameters entail a validation-optimal number of epochs in [0, 4], implying termination before the end of a six-epoch warm-up. A validation-selected six-configuration search improves fine-tuning, but the supervised models remain superior. Freezing additional blocks and decreasing learning rates may reduce overfitting, in the context of fitting on $\sim$230 trials.

\subsection{Task Dependence and Robustness}\label{sec:robust}

On the two-class BNCI2014-004 dataset, fine-tuned CBraMod (0.8085) scores below the best performing supervised arm (0.8458), though this difference was not statistically detectable, and above EEG Conformer (0.7981). The uniform four-class deficit is therefore not reproduced; this cannot be attributed to the change in classes alone, which differs alongside channels, participants, and protocol, relative to BCI IV-2a.

A triple reseed of the full pipeline, comparing fine-tuned CBraMod with random initialization, found accuracies superior by 0.130--0.139 on BCI IV-2a and 0.107--0.111 on BNCI2014-004, in all cases with $\geq$8/9 subject-level wins. Rerunning on CPU, with an identical architecture on a deterministic backend, preserved the advantage.

Extending training to 240 epochs, for both arms, improved the accuracy of the random initialization by only 0.017, with best validation Brier still near chance (0.755 vs.\ 0.750; Fig.~\ref{fig:curve}).

The unscaled foundation-model logits are typically overconfident. Fitted validation temperatures ranged from 3.1 to 9.5 for the frozen configurations, with the maximum temperature (20.0) reached for some subjects. After scaling, CBraMod exhibited an ECE of 0.103--0.124 (Table~\ref{tab:calib}), within the 0.101--0.143 range of the eight single-window supervised models, despite the reduced accuracy and worse Brier score. Temperature scaling improved the Brier score of the pretrained model only modestly, compared to a reduction in ECE from 0.464 to 0.099 for a randomly initialized model.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}

The central methodological observation is not that one input pipeline is universally better. Matched-input estimates range from a 0.078 improvement for ATCNet to a 0.088 reduction for EEG Conformer. Although no individual term survives correction, direct subject-paired contrasts distinguish ATCNet from the other architectures. These findings suggest that decomposing performance gaps using a single comparator may be unreliable; matched-input differences should instead be reported across multiple architectures.

Our results do not imply that pretrained EEG encoders fail generally. Under a validation-locked cross-session protocol, both evaluated encoders trail supervised architectures on four-class BCI IV-2a, while fine-tuned CBraMod is competitive on BNCI2014-004. Temperature scaling improves calibration despite persistent accuracy deficits.

Several limitations remain. We evaluate two foundation models and two datasets, with only nine subjects per dataset. BNCI2014-004 differs in class count, electrode count, and recording protocol, preventing controlled attribution of task-dependent differences. Matched-input comparisons use narrow hyperparameter searches and one split and seed per arm; although ShallowConvNet retains its sign across three CPU seeds, the other architectures were not reseeded. Thus, the cross-architecture findings remain exploratory pending replication across splits and seeds.
