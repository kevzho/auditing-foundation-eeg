Large pretrained encoders have become the default proposal for EEG decoding.
LaBraM~\cite{jiang2024labram} and CBraMod~\cite{wang2025cbramod} are trained on
thousands of hours of unlabelled recordings and offered as general-purpose
feature extractors, following the pattern that made pretraining effective in
vision and language. Motor imagery --- decoding an imagined limb movement from
scalp EEG --- is the clinical entry point for non-invasive brain--computer
interfaces, and it is where that proposal matters most: a subject supplies only
a few hundred labelled trials, so if pretraining at scale transfers here, it
changes what a usable interface costs to calibrate. Whether it transfers is
contested, and recent benchmarks
disagree~\cite{kontras2026neuroatlas,zare2026negativecontrol}.

The literature cannot currently settle the question, for two reasons. The first
is protocol. Published four-class accuracies on the standard benchmark span
roughly 68\% to 91\%, and the spread tracks evaluation protocol at least as
much as architecture: results under the official cross-session split cluster at
68--75\%, while reports above 85\% generally involve within-session random
splits, augmentation applied before partitioning, or unstated checkpoint
selection~\cite{yu2022lffn}. Numbers produced under these protocols are not
commensurable with one another, and are frequently tabulated as though they
were.

The second reason is input. A pretrained encoder and a task-specific decoder
must be fed different preprocessing --- each receives the band and sampling
rate it was built for --- so any measured gap between them mixes the model with
the pipeline. The largest benchmark in the field names this as a confounder but
does not isolate it~\cite{kontras2026neuroatlas}. The obvious remedy is a
matched-input control: retrain the task-specific decoder on the arrays the
pretrained model actually consumes. We run that control on three architectures
and find that it is not stable: the measured term differs in sign depending on
which comparator is used.

This paper contributes: (i) a validation-locked evaluation protocol whose
leakage guard is a property of the artifact format rather than of author
discipline; (ii) an audit of two pretrained EEG encoders against three
supervised architectures under three adaptation modes and an
architecture-matched random-initialisation control; (iii) the finding that the
matched-input term differs in sign across comparator architectures and is not
reliably estimable from any one of them; (iv) evidence that the resulting deficit is specific to the four-class
setting and absent on two classes; and (v) the observation that a single
temperature fitted on validation data returns these encoders to supervised-range
calibration despite far lower accuracy.
